110 850 cases and 118 deaths – as of October 4th, 2026, the dengue cases reported in Cambodia according to the World Health Organization (WHO) was a 138 percent increase in comparison to the previous year of 2025. Despite years of prevention efforts, dengue continues to pose a public-health challenge, raising questions about how mosquitoes spread the disease and what more can be done to protect communities. 

Science

Through the interview of Kiripost and Dr. Sébastien Boyer, Head of Medical and Veterinary Entomology at the Institut Pasteur du Cambodge, said “the only factors we can definitively say attract mosquitoes are carbon dioxide released by people and lactic acid, the chemical that builds up when you exercise. However, there are more than 3500 species of mosquitoes in the world and they are all different from one another and are attracted to different factors.

Yet, scientifically, these cues do not fully explain why some people are more attractive to mosquitoes than others. Although studies have been done all over different parts of the world, it seems as though no one’s species is the same as the other. International studies have identified multiple cues on how mosquitoes locate human hosts, however how these findings apply to Cambodia remains uncertain.”
 

Cambodia: Does attraction mean high risks?

“A higher attraction from mosquitoes alone does not necessarily mean that someone is at a higher risk of contracting dengue at all. 

A mosquito must first be infected with dengue virus, and transmission also depends on factors such as mosquito abundance, infection prevalence, human behaviour and environmental conditions.

This distinction matters because research into mosquito attraction should not lead people to believe that some individuals are simply destined to contract dengue. Instead, it highlights how complicated the interaction between humans and mosquitoes can be and understanding these interactions may eventually help researchers develop new approaches to mosquito control and prevention of mosquito-borne diseases.”, he answered.

Prevention

In Cambodia, where dengue transmission remains a recurring public-health challenge, public-health guidance recommends reducing exposure to mosquito bites by using effective insect repellents, wearing clothing that covers exposed skin and reducing contact with mosquitoes around homes.

Research may explain why mosquitoes seem to favour some people, but preventing dengue still depends on reducing opportunities for infected mosquitoes to bite people in the first place. 

However, does everyone have the ability to follow advice that is dependent on more than awareness alone? For families with young children and elderly seniors living at home, who takes on the responsibility to keep the house rid of mosquito breeding sites and keep the house protected from mosquitoes? 

The experience of a family from Battambang, whose nine-year-old son was hospitalized with dengue, offers a starting point for examining these questions. The family consists of two elderly grandparents, a widower, and two young children; although their circumstances do not establish that they lack access to prevention, they highlight why public-health advice must be considered alongside the realities of family life. 

Beyond Preventions

The fight with dengue does not end with just preventing the mosquitoes biting us, it also includes the care that patients receive at the hospital, this includes timely diagnosis and appropriate care for patients.

Although the development of medical equipment for detecting and diagnosing dengue is much more advanced than before, access to and knowledge of these equipment is still limited in Cambodia. Most especially for families from provinces who have to travel to Phnom Penh in order to be timely treated. 

Taking the family from Battambang, seeking treatment for their nine years old grandson, meant travelling to Phnom Penh after four days with no improvement at the local hospital. Grandma and Grandpa spent 100$ taking a car arriving at the National Pediatric Hospital at 3 a.m on 16th October 2026 morning. 

“When we got here, there was barely any bed for him as there were lots of patients. Then his brother and I slept in the yard near the room while his grandpa was looking after him inside, but since there was rain last night, we had to move.”, said the grandma.
